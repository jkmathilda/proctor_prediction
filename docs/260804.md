## 2. Best Model on Kaggle — MICE + GPR/GBT Blend (v15)

**Correction from an earlier pass on this doc**: internal OOF numbers pointed at model1i as the strongest general-model candidate, but the actual Kaggle leaderboard says otherwise — **v15 is the best submission so far at 0.2383**, narrowly ahead of v10 (0.2384) and clearly ahead of the general-model family (model1i 0.2610, model1 0.2663).

v15's architecture: an **ARD-kernel Gaussian Process** (Matern ν=2.5 + WhiteKernel, per-feature length-scale, feature pruning — see Section 4) blended with a **HistGradientBoostingRegressor** (GBT), on MICE-imputed engineered features, with `hyd_cond_hyd_gradient` dropped outright (Section 6). The GP/GBT blend weight is a fixed per-target constant (GP share 0.9 for MDD, 0.35 for OWC) rather than an optimized weight — simpler than model1i's SLSQP-fit 3-way blend, and it still wins.

**Why it works better than the general-model family:**
- **MICE recovers real signal that the no-imputation model (model1) forfeits.** §7 of `data_analysis.md` shows the high-missingness lab columns are reconstructable from the universal feature set with usable accuracy (LOI OOF R²=0.807, kf OOF R²=0.754) — every MICE-imputed model in this project (model1i, v10, v15, v16) beats model1's no-imputation baseline on Kaggle (0.2663).
- **Blending two models with different error profiles beats either alone, consistently, across this whole project.** `data_analysis.md` §8 (Ridge+GPR+XGBoost: 0.850/0.805 vs. best single model XGBoost 0.835/0.797), v5's 0.8·GBM+0.2·PINN blend, and v15's own GP+GBT blend (0.2037 internal vs. GP-alone 0.2166 / GBT-alone 0.2359) all show the same pattern: a kernel-smooth GP and a tree-split GBT cancel out some of each other's mistakes.
- **ARD's built-in feature selection matters at this dataset size.** v15's GP prunes ~47 engineered features down to 13 per target (Section 4) before fitting — likely part of why it generalizes better than model1i's blend, which uses the full `IMPUTED_FEATURES` set without pruning.
- **Open question, not yet resolved**: why does the leaner GP+GBT blend beat the 3-model Ridge+GPR+XGBoost blend on the real test set, when model1i's *internal* OOF (0.242) looked competitive with v15's *internal* single-split estimate (0.204)? The most likely explanation is the validation-gap finding in Section 1 — v15's single-split estimate is more optimistic than model1i's 5-fold estimate, so the "internal OOF" column understates how close they actually are; the true gap on Kaggle (0.2610 vs 0.2383) is real, but the internal numbers alone would not have predicted its size.

**Ridge's role**: in the general-model family, Ridge adds essentially nothing once GPR+XGBoost are present, especially for OWC (model1i's OWC blend weight: `ridge=0.000, gpr=0.038, xgboost=0.962`) — consistent with `data_analysis.md` §2's finding that OWC is poorly explained linearly. v15 skips Ridge entirely, which may also be part of why it edges out the general-model family.

## 3. Splits

Three different CV/validation schemes have been used across this project, and they are not interchangeable:

1. **Plain shuffled `KFold`** (v1–v6, and model1/model1i/model2i's internal 5-fold OOF) — no stratification. `data_analysis.md` §2 found this can concentrate rare soil compositions or produce unlucky folds (one OWC linear-regression fold hit val R² = −1.45).
2. **Soil-composition-stratified `KFold`** (v7 onward, v14) — buckets rows by dominant DIN fraction (Sand/Gravel/Fine) and stratifies on that, fixing the fold-composition instability found above.
3. **Density-quantile `StratifiedShuffleSplit`** (v10/v15/v16) — 80/20 holdout stratified by target-density quantile bin; `--folds` controls how many independent splits are drawn and averaged.

**The v15 vs. v16 comparison is the clearest evidence in this repo that single-split validation reads optimistic.** v15 (1 split) reports blend NMAE 0.2037; v16 — same architecture, same feature set, same `hyd_grad` drop — averaged over 3 splits reports **0.2515 ± 0.034**, with individual splits ranging from 0.2063 to 0.2883. A single split can land anywhere in that range by chance; only the multi-split mean gives a sense of the true variance. The same pattern shows up between v10's single-split nosplit run (0.1976) and its own multi-split variants. Going forward, any new NMAE number from the GPR family should be reported as a multi-split mean ± std, not a single-split point estimate.

## 4. ARD for GPR

v10/v15/v16 use an ARD (Automatic Relevance Determination) kernel for the Gaussian Process: `Matern(ν=2.5)` with a **separate length-scale per feature**, plus a `WhiteKernel` for noise, instead of one shared length-scale (`--isotropic` is the opt-out). This does two things at once:

- **Soft, built-in feature selection.** After fitting, features whose learned length-scale exceeds 100 (i.e. the GP found them irrelevant — the kernel barely varies as that feature changes) are dropped, with a floor of at least 5 kept, and the GP is refit on the surviving subset. In v15's run this narrowed ~47 engineered features down to 13 per target.
- **Per-target relevance differs meaningfully.** MDD leans on gradation/PSD-size features (`psd_C_U`, `psd_size_at_d10_mm`, `psd_fraction_sand`) and Atterberg plasticity; OWC leans more on fines content and the fine-fraction-specific columns (`psd_passing_at_0_063mm_pct`, `atterberg_plastic_limit_pct`, `loss_on_ignition_pct`). This matches `data_analysis.md` §4's XGBoost gain-importance finding that the two targets are driven by different parts of the PSD curve.

**Rationale for a GP over a neural net here** (from `v10.py`'s own docstring): at ~200 training rows, a GP is data-efficient and returns calibrated per-sample uncertainty, whereas a neural net (already tried in v3/v4) needs more data than this dataset provides. Gradient-boosted trees are the other strong small-data choice, which is why the GP is paired with one (HistGBT) rather than used alone.

## 5. Imputation (MICE, Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations)

Every script from v6 onward, plus model1i/model2i, imputes the high-missingness columns (Atterberg limits, `hyd_cond_kf_m_s`, `loss_on_ignition_pct`) via scikit-learn's `IterativeImputer` (MICE) with an `ExtraTreesRegressor` per-column estimator, fit **fold-isolated** (train-fold only, applied to validation/test — never refit on held-out data) to avoid leakage. model1i additionally fits the imputer **separately for fine- vs. coarse-grained rows**, since Atterberg limits are only physically measured for fine-grained soils — imputing them for coarse-grained rows would be inventing a lab measurement that structurally doesn't apply.

MICE  fills missing values by modeling each incomplete variable as a function of the others, one variable at a time. It cycles through the columns: temporarily fill everything with placeholders, then for each variable in turn, regress it on all other variables using the observed rows and draw new imputed values from that fitted model. It repeats this cycle 10–20 times (10 cycles in our versions which is default) and lets the imputations converge to a stable joint distribution.

Note for correctness of imputations: Likely the imputed values are wrong, however What MICE preserves is the distribution, ie means, variances, covariances, and regression coefficients estimated from the completed data are approximately unbiased, with standard errors. 


### Imputation reliability

Before trusting imputed values, `data_analysis.md` §7 tested whether `hyd_cond_kf_m_s` and `loss_on_ignition_pct` can actually be reconstructed from the universal feature set (5-fold OOF XGBoost, log10-transformed, evaluated only on the ~61 rows where each is genuinely observed):

| Column | OOF R² (log10) | Median error factor | 75th %ile error factor |
|---|---|---|---|
| `loss_on_ignition_pct` | 0.807 | ~1.26× | ~1.4× |
| `hyd_cond_kf_m_s` | 0.754 | ~3.5× | ~14.7× (worst ≈ 3637×) |

LOI reconstructs tightly and is a safe imputation candidate. kf's reconstruction is heavy-tailed — usable on average but a meaningful minority of predictions miss by an order of magnitude or more, since kf itself spans ~7 orders of magnitude in this dataset. This is why every MICE-consuming script also adds `*_was_missing` / `*_is_missing` indicator flags alongside the imputed values: the model can learn to discount a fabricated kf point estimate rather than treat it as a precise lab measurement.

## 6. Not Including `hyd_cond_hyd_gradient`

v15/v16 drop `hyd_cond_hyd_gradient` outright (not imputed, not used as a predictor at all) — the one deliberate deviation from v10. Two things motivate this:

- **70% missing, always paired with `hyd_cond_kf_m_s`** (same rows missing both), per `docs/02-design/features/proctor-prediction.design.md`.
- `data_analysis.md` §6: "Rows missing `hyd_cond_hyd_gradient` don't show a systematic shift in any model's relative residual distribution — dropping/imputing these columns doesn't appear to bias predictions for the remaining models."

**Open tension, and how the real Kaggle scores actually settle it**: v10's own ARD-GP run (before the drop) found `hyd_cond_hyd_gradient` *did* survive OWC's feature-relevance selection — length-scale 7.14, inside the top-10 most-relevant OWC features. So the drop trades away a feature the GP itself found informative, in exchange for one fewer imputed/uncertain column. Internally, v15's single-split estimate (0.2037) even reads slightly *worse* than v10's (0.1976), which would suggest the drop hurts.

But v10 and v15 are otherwise architecturally identical and were both scored on the same held-out Kaggle test set — the one clean, apples-to-apples comparison available for this question. **v10 (keeps `hyd_grad`): 0.2384. v15 (drops it): 0.2383.** The difference is 0.0001 — indistinguishable from noise. This matches `data_analysis.md` §6's original finding almost exactly: dropping/imputing `hyd_cond_hyd_gradient` doesn't meaningfully bias predictions either way. The internal single-split numbers disagreeing with each other (and with the Kaggle tie) is itself further evidence for Section 3's point — a single 80/20 split just doesn't have the resolution to detect a real effect this small, let alone a non-effect.

## 7. Ongoing Work — General Model + Correctional Model

**General model — model1i.** Ridge+GPR+XGBoost blend on MICE-imputed `IMPUTED_FEATURES`, fine/coarse-split imputation. This is the best out-of-fold result on the full dataset among the general-model-only family (MDD R²=0.869, OWC R²=0.809), and the best-scoring member of that family on Kaggle (0.2610) — though note from Section 2 that the GPR/GBT family (v15) still beats it outright.

**Correctional model — model2i.** Wraps model1i with a second-stage residual correction on fine-grained rows only (n=89), using `data_analysis.ipynb`'s "Group C: Full specialist" feature set (MICE-completed Atterberg limits/PI/LOI/kf + missingness flags + the general model's own OOF prediction as a feature). Originally MDD-only (`MDDFineGrainedResidualCorrector`, matching the notebook's own validated result), the script has since been extended to also correct OWC on the same fine-grained rows (`OWCFineGrainedResidualCorrector`) — this OWC correction has no notebook precedent; it's this repo's own extension, validated only against its own nested OOF numbers. Both correctors compare three candidates per run — XGBoost (Optuna-tuned), a GPR, and a weighted ensemble — and keep whichever wins on nested OOF MSE. Coarse-grained rows (n=112) are untouched for both targets — Atterberg/LOI/kf aren't physically measured for coarse soils.

- **Nested internal result**: fine-grained MDD subset R² 0.807 → 0.826; fine-grained OWC subset R² 0.767 → 0.779.
- **Kaggle result: 0.2667 — worse than model1i's 0.2610.** This is a real negative result, not just an unvalidated one: the correction improves the nested OOF metric it was fit to optimize, but makes the actual held-out predictions worse overall.
- **Why it likely doesn't generalize**: the residual the correctors are fitting is thin to begin with — nested OOF R² on the residual itself is 0.05–0.08 for MDD's candidates and 0.04 (XGBoost) down to −0.38 (GPR) for OWC's, on an already-small n=89 subset further split for nested validation. A second model layered on top of that weak a signal, at that sample size, is exactly the kind of small-n overfitting this project has flagged before (KNN in v7, the shared multi-output MLP in Section 8, single-split GPR validation in Section 3).
- **Status**: `model2i.py`, `models/model2i.joblib`, and `submissions/submission_model2i.csv` are untracked in git as of this run. Given the Kaggle regression, the OWC correction in particular should probably be reverted or re-validated (e.g. with a proper held-out split rather than the same nested-OOF procedure that already looked favorable) before it's treated as an improvement.

## 8. Combined vs. Separate Model (MDD, OWC)

Every shipped model in this project trains MDD and OWC as **two fully independent models** — this was tested, not assumed.

**OWC/MDD dependence testing** (`data_analysis.md` §9):
- Raw targets are strongly negatively correlated: **r = −0.768** (physically expected — a denser compaction optimum goes with a lower optimum water content).
- Even after each target gets its own best-tuned blend, the *residuals* are still meaningfully correlated: **r = −0.474** — motivating a test of whether chaining one target's prediction into the other's inputs would recover that leftover shared signal.

**Architectures compared** (5-fold OOF, same feature set):

| Architecture | MDD R² | OWC R² |
|---|---|---|
| **Separate Ridge+GPR+XGBoost blend** | **0.850** | **0.805** |
| OWC → MDD chain (predicted OWC as MDD feature) | 0.830 | — |
| MDD → OWC chain (predicted MDD as OWC feature) | — | 0.787 |
| Independent XGBoost, no chaining | 0.827 | 0.788 |
| Separate MLPs (2 independent networks) | 0.179 | 0.709 |
| Shared multi-output MLP (1 network, 2 outputs) | 0.097 | **−0.151** |

Chaining doesn't help — it performs about the same as or slightly worse than not chaining. The shared multi-output MLP is the worst model tested (negative OWC R²); its own cross-output residual correlation (r = −0.888) suggests it mostly learns the MDD/OWC trade-off axis instead of each target's independent signal. **Conclusion: independent per-target models is the best architecture found for this dataset** — which is why model1/model1i/model2i and the v10/v15/v16 family all fit MDD and OWC separately rather than jointly.

## 9. New, In Progress — Curve-LSTM (`eda.ipynb`, "Using the Curve")

A different idea from everything above: instead of feeding the grain-size distribution (PSD) in as hand-picked scalar summary points (`psd_size_at_d10_mm`, `d60`, etc. — what every model in Sections 1–8 uses), treat the **11 PSD percentile points (D10→D98) as a curve** and encode the whole curve with a shared LSTM. Adapted from TUHH's own "Soil Parameter Prediction" repo, which does the same thing for stress–strain curves.

**Architecture — `SharedEncoderTwoHead`:**
- Each sample's curve is `(percentile, log10(diameter_mm))` pairs, 11 points, percentile normalized to [0,1] and diameter log-transformed (it spans ~5 orders of magnitude).
- One shared LSTM (`lstm_units=[32]` by default) encodes the curve; two separate heads (MDD, OWC) pool the LSTM's per-point hidden states and pass the result through their own small dense stack (`dense_units=[16]`, dropout 0.2).
- **The pooling is the actual idea**: instead of last-timestep or mean pooling, each head pools using **fixed, per-target sensitivity weights** — so the curve segments that most move with that target dominate what the head sees, rather than treating every percentile equally.
- Sensitivity weights come from `curve_point_sensitivity()`: for each of the 11 curve points, a standardized partial-regression coefficient of that point on the target, controlling for the *other* target. TUHH's original method pivots a synthetic swept dataset (repeated controls, one variable varied at a time) to get a clean derivative; this dataset has no repeated controls (201 real lab samples), so the partial-regression coefficient is used as the closest observational-data analogue — conceptually the same "how much does the curve move per unit of the target, holding the other fixed" question, estimated rather than measured directly.

**Extension — `SharedEncoderScalarTwoHead`.** Concatenates the 17 (MICE-imputed) scalar classification features — fines fractions, mold diameter, grading-shape coefficients, imputed kf/LOI — onto each head's pooled curve representation before its dense layers, since those aren't points along the curve and can't be fed through the LSTM directly. Bumps dropout/weight-decay up (0.2→0.3, 1e-4→5e-4) to compensate for the higher params-to-rows ratio a 32-dim pooled vector + 17 scalars creates at n=201.
